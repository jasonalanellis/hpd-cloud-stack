3. Verify Licensing and Insurance

This step is non-negotiable. * License Verification: Go directly to the CSLB website (cslb.ca.gov) and input the contractor's license number. Confirm it's active, the classification matches your project (e.g., B for General Building Contractor, C-10 for Electrical, C-36 for Plumbing), and there are no disciplinary actions. An expired or suspended license is an immediate red flag. * Insurance Verification: Request current Certificates of Insurance (COI) directly from the contractor's insurance provider, not just a copy from the contractor. You need to verify: * General Liability Insurance: This protects you from property damage or injury caused by the contractor or their crew. Ensure coverage is robust, typically $1M per occurrence. * Worker's Compensation Insurance: If the contractor has employees, this is legally required. It protects you from liability if an employee is injured on your property. Without it, you could be held responsible for medical bills and lost wages. * Bonding: A contractor's bond offers financial protection if they fail to complete the project or pay suppliers. Verify the bond is active and sufficient for your project size.

4. Review Past Work and References

A contractor's portfolio should showcase projects similar to yours. Ask for at least three recent references and actually call them. Ask specific questions: * Was the project completed on time and within the agreed-upon flat-rate? * How did they handle unexpected issues or change orders? * Was communication clear and consistent? * Would you hire them again? * Did they maintain a clean and safe worksite?

5. Obtain Detailed Bids and Contracts

Once you have a shortlist, request comprehensive, itemized bids from at least three contractors. These bids should include: * Detailed scope of work. * Specific materials to be used (brand, model, quantity). * Payment schedule (typically milestones, not large upfront payments). * Start and completion dates. * Warranty information. * Procedures for change orders.

A well-drafted contract protects both parties. Never sign a contract with blank spaces. Ensure it includes a lien waiver clause to protect you from subcontractors or suppliers placing a lien on your property if the general contractor fails to pay them.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: What's the biggest risk of hiring an unlicensed contractor?

A: The most significant risks are lack of recourse for poor workmanship, no insurance coverage if someone gets injured or your property is damaged, and potential legal and financial liability for you as the homeowner. You might also have difficulty selling your home if unpermitted work is discovered.

Q: How do I know if a contractor's license is appropriate for my project?

A: The CSLB website lists license classifications. A "B" license is for General Building Contractors, suitable for most large remodels or additions. Specialized trades like plumbing (C-36) or electrical (C-10) require specific licenses. Ensure the contractor's license matches the primary work scope. When you need to find licensed home contractors near me, matching their license to the job is paramount.

Q: What should I do if a contractor asks for a large upfront payment?

A: In California, it's illegal for a contractor to ask for more than 10% of the contract price or $1,000, whichever is less, as a down payment. If a contractor demands more, consider it a major red flag and look elsewhere. This regulation protects homeowners from unscrupulous practices.

Q: Can I pull my own permits to save money?

A: While possible for certain minor projects, pulling your own permits for significant work is generally ill-advised. It shifts all liability and responsibility for code compliance onto you. A reputable contractor will handle all permitting, ensuring the work meets all local San Diego building codes and inspections.
